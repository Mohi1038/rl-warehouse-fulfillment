01 · The floor

A 21 × 15 warehouse

Shelves are obstacles you pick from the aisle beside them. Three docks sit along the bottom wall, a charger in the corner. One tick is one cell of travel; a shift lasts 720 ticks.

02 · Orders

Demand arrives in waves

Orders arrive by a non-homogeneous Poisson process: a quiet start, a midday peak and one random rush. Each holds 1–3 items on “hot” shelves. Express orders are worth about 3× bulk; rare VIP orders (★) are worth 5× with tight deadlines.

03 · Trucks

Trucks don’t wait

Every dock has a truck leaving every 120–180 ticks. The ring is its countdown — but the real departure is jittered and hidden from the agent. Miss it and an express order is lost; standard and bulk orders move to the next truck at half value.

04 · The robot

Capacity, battery, one decision at a time

The robot carries 6 weight units, drains its battery as it moves (the ring around it) and must keep enough charge to reach the charger. It is only asked what to do when it becomes free.

05 · The whole shift

Scroll to run the clock

This is the trained policy on held-out seed 100026 — a typical shift, not a hand-picked one. It ends with 65.9% of the revenue captured, against 56.7% for the Greedy baseline on the same seed.

The pressure

Why one robot can’t serve everyone

The line is the arrival rate λ(t) for this shift; each dot is a real order, coloured by tier and sized by value. At the peak, orders arrive faster than one robot can travel between shelves and docks — so the job is triage: deciding what to let go.

06 · Observation

The open orders, as the agent sees them

At a real decision in this shift, these are the orders in the robot’s 16 slots: value, tier, dock, slack until the truck, items left, distance, whether it fits, and age.

07 · Tokens

Orders become a set of tokens

Each order becomes a 14-feature token. A Transformer encoder treats them as a set — no order is first — so the same policy runs unchanged with 24 or 32 slots.

08 · Attention

Attention decides what matters

A learned [CLS] token attends over every order. Beam width is the real attention weight from the trained policy at this decision. On average express orders get 2× their uniform share.

09 · Action

A pointer picks one order

A pointer head scores each order slot; a small head scores DELIVER, CHARGE and WAIT. An action mask removes anything invalid or unsafe. The highlighted order is the one the policy actually chose.

The breakthrough

How far ahead should the agent care?

Actions take different amounts of time, so the textbook choice discounts by γτ per tick. With γ = 0.995 that fades within a few dozen decisions — 26 on this shift. Discounting per decision keeps the whole shift in view — and trained far better.

10 · Imagining futures

Before acting, imagine 32 futures

At each decision the planner tries every valid action on 32 sampled futures — new order arrivals, new truck jitter — and finishes each with the policy. It never sees the real future.

11 · Scoring

Every branch gets a score

Ghost paths are the candidate actions; the bars are the mean revenue each one reached across the sampled futures.

12 · Overruling the policy

Sometimes it disagrees with PPO

Here the look-ahead overrules the policy’s first choice. Over 100 held-out seeds this lifts revenue from 66.4% to 71.0% (paired +4.6 ± 1.7) for the same checkpoint.

Scaling out

One shared brain, three robots

The same simulator runs robots asynchronously: whichever robot frees up next asks the shared policy what to do. Orders are claimed so no two robots chase the same one. On this shift three robots capture 98.8%. (The fleet policies were trained with the original γτ discounting.)

What this is — and isn’t

Honest limits

A simulator

Demand, layouts and reward weights are modelled assumptions, not measurements from a live facility.

The future is the real limit

With Greedy finishing the simulations, look-ahead given the true future reaches 72.0% on the first 20 seeds, against 63.1% with sampled futures.

Planning costs compute

Look-ahead takes about 3 s per decision; the policy alone decides in milliseconds.

Fleet coordination

The three-robot result uses a shared policy with order claiming — evidence of scale, not optimal multi-agent coordination.
